# Oversupply, "popularity" and price dynamics for the RotaSat score (Campo de Cartagena horticulture)

Research date: 2026-11-14. Scope: evidence and a defensible formula for the `(1 - oversupply)` term in `score ~ success% x price x (1 - oversupply)` (T-012), plus the concrete price/area series to feed it. Price forecasting is NOT redone (rejected in `prices-and-decision-layer.md` Q1; week-of-year P10/P50/P90 stays).

Labels: "(VERIFIED)" = I opened the page/PDF or ran the query on this machine today. "(SEARCH-ONLY)" = only seen in a search summary. "(MY COMPUTATION)" = I downloaded Eurostat data and ran the numbers myself (method in the Appendix); those are findings of this note, not of the cited source, and are flagged as such.

**Headline for the team (read this first):**
1. Evidence that area drives price exists in the Spanish literature (supply response to expected price is real, short-run area elasticities 0.4 to 1.5 for greenhouse vegetables), but **at national annual level I could not detect an area-to-price effect for lettuce, broccoli/cauliflower, melon, almond, orange, mandarin or lemon** (all standard errors larger than the estimates). Weather/yield, EU-wide supply and demand dominate year-to-year price swings (annual sd of log real price 0.22 to 0.38).
2. The market is **European, not local**: Spain is ~42% of EU lettuce output, extra-EU imports are <1% of EU output, and ~80% of exported Murcian lettuce is eaten in the EU. The Campo de Cartagena is a large share of that (my estimate ~20% of EU lettuce on an annual basis, higher in winter), so comarca area is not irrelevant, but one farmer's choice is ~0.
3. Therefore the honest oversupply term is a **small, uncertain price shift** driven by an area anomaly index, with the effect size drawn as an uncertain parameter in the Monte Carlo, plus a visible "crowded crop" flag. It should not be a hard multiplicative penalty in [0,1]. Pooled anomaly slope from my regressions is about -0.3 (+/- 0.3 naive), i.e. a +20% area anomaly costs roughly -6% price, small next to the 22-38% inter-annual price sd.

## Q1. Cobweb / hog-cycle dynamics and price elasticities in Spanish fruit and vegetables

### Takeaway
Spanish horticulture does show area-supply response to expected prices (Galdeano-Gomez 2001: short-run area elasticities 0.42-1.52, long-run 0.53-2.34, against demand elasticities of only -0.06 to -0.34, so supply/demand elasticity ratios of 2.7-18.8 which in textbook cobweb theory means explosive price swings unless expectations are better than naive lagged prices; the paper finds farmers do use more than lagged prices). But the effect is for greenhouse crops in Almeria/Granada (1975-1999). For open-field Murcia lettuce/broccoli I found no estimated cobweb, and my own national-level test finds area changes respond weakly or not at all to the previous year's price change (only cauliflower+broccoli: +0.14 +/- 0.05). Perennials (lemon, almond, citrus) show slow, multi-year oversupply driven by planting waves and world supply, not annual cobwebs.

### Cited Findings
- Cobweb logic: a time lag between supply decision and market sale makes agricultural prices cycle; refinements say farmers use several years of prices (Coase & Fowler), which damps the cycle. A classic lettuce acreage model uses prices lagged one year and prior-year acreage (US, 1960-74); it also notes shippers control a large share of acreage, complicating the simple story. — [Cobweb model (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobweb_model); [AgEcon lettuce acreage record](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/32169/files/01010161.pdf) (SEARCH-ONLY, via search summary)
- **Spain, greenhouse vegetables SE Spain 1975-1999** (pepper, tomato, cucumber, courgette, aubergine; Almeria and Granada provinces): area function `log S_t = a + b log P_expected + c log S_(t-1) + ...` (Nerlove partial adjustment). Short-run (c/p) area-price elasticities: pepper 0.62, tomato 0.56, cucumber 0.90, courgette 1.52, aubergine 0.42; long-run (l/p): pepper 0.84, tomato 1.56, cucumber 0.98, courgette 2.34, aubergine 0.53. Demand own-price elasticities (c/p): pepper -0.22, tomato -0.06, cucumber -0.17, courgette -0.34, aubergine -0.23. Supply-to-demand ratio [EO/ED]: pepper 4.18, tomato 18.83, cucumber 6.53, courgette 13.26, aubergine 2.69. Expectations are a composite of lagged prices and rational (current-information) components for pepper, tomato, courgette, cucumber; aubergine only lagged prices. The author notes that price change "although important, is not enough to explain" the observed area expansion in some products (tomato, aubergine), pointing to other factors. — [Galdeano-Gomez (2001), Economia Agraria y Recursos Naturales 1(2):21-41 (VERIFIED, PDF read)](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/28742/files/01020021.pdf); same author's follow-up finds current market information used alongside lagged prices (composite rational expectations) — [Galdeano-Gomez 2007, Agribusiness 23(1)](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:wly:agribz:v:23:y:2007:i:1:p:57-83) (SEARCH-ONLY)
- **Spain, household demand 1987-1999** (inverse demand system, MAPA consumption data; fresh vegetables: tomato, pepper, green bean, brassicas, onion, other). Own-price (price-flexibility) coefficients, all significant and negative: tomato -0.23, pepper -0.18, green bean -0.20, brassicas -0.24, onion -0.22, other vegetables -0.29. These are flexibilities of the *normalised* price (price / total expenditure) with respect to quantity, estimated on retail/household consumption, not farm-gate prices. Scale flexibilities: tomato -0.90, pepper -0.82, green bean -0.94, brassicas -1.10, onion -1.74. The authors' own reading: "price adjustments are in general small" for a given quantity change. Lettuce, broccoli, melon, watermelon are not separate categories. — [Galdeano-Gomez & Jaen-Garcia (2003), REEAP 198, Cuadro 9 (VERIFIED, PDF read)](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/184184/files/pdf_reeap-r198_04.pdf)
- French reference range: price elasticities of demand for fruit and vegetables as a whole generally between -0.5 and -1; summer fruit demand responds to price except melons (weather matters more). — [CTIFL, Determinants of demand for fruit and vegetables](https://www.ctifl.fr/en/determinants-of-demand-for-fruit-and-vegetables-infos-ctifl-408) (SEARCH-ONLY)
- US review: Okrent & Alston (2011, USDA ERS / UC Davis) re-estimate demand for fruit and vegetables; an eight-vegetable study finds all own-price elasticities except cabbage negative, below 1 in absolute value; a 2015 consumer-panel thesis finds leafy-green categories own-price elastic (conflicting). Commodity-level lettuce/broccoli/melon values were NOT retrieved. — [UC Davis cwe1002](https://mondavi.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/291/2011/04/cwe1002.pdf), [Arizona thesis](https://repository.arizona.edu/handle/10150/628190) (SEARCH-ONLY); the USDA ERS Commodity and Food Elasticities Database is the direct route to per-commodity numbers.
- Spanish consumption descriptives (not elasticities): household melon+watermelon consumption +8.6% to May 2025 while average price -6.4% to 1.20 EUR/kg; watermelon demand -8.2% in 2022-23 when price rose sharply; lettuce/escarole/endive demand -3.7% in H1 2026. — [FreshPlaza](https://www.freshplaza.com/europe/article/9758006/spanish-household-melon-and-watermelon-consumption-grows-by-8-6-percent), [Fruit Today](https://fruittoday.com/en/fruit-and-vegetable-consumption-falls-by-2-8-in-spanish-households/)
- **Open-field winter vegetables, within-season supply timing matters more than annual area.** Cajamar (using MAPA Avances): in 2021 national lettuce area was 33.7 kha (-0.9%) but production 1,077 kt (+12.0%); broccoli area 35.9 kha (+12.2%), production 561 kt (-5.0%); cauliflower 8.8 kha, artichoke 15.2 kha. Broccoli and cauliflower had the least favourable export performance in 2021 "because of supply accumulation at specific moments of the production period when it exceeded demand". In 2021/22 weather staggered supply and cut volume, lifting lettuce prices +18.2% to +52.9% vs the previous campaign (iceberg/romaine/baby), >25% above the 5-campaign mean; broccoli +5% and cauliflower +7% average price Sept-April; artichoke 107.7 EUR/100 kg, +9.8% vs 5-campaign mean (but -5.9% vs previous). — [Cajamar, Horticolas aire libre C1 2022 (VERIFIED, PDF is AES-encrypted but pdftotext reads it)](https://publicacionescajamar.es/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/horticolas-aire-libre-c1-2022.pdf)
- **Lemon crisis.** ASAJA Alicante describes campaign 2018-19 as a "cataclysm of origin prices" (a "perfect storm": much larger harvest plus drought), says new Vega Baja plantations were encouraged by earlier good prices; a snippet reads "of 6,000 ha planted in the Vega Baja, 5,000 ha were destroyed" (ambiguous in the extract; verify before quoting). — [ASAJA Balance Agrario 2019](https://www.asaja.com/files/noticia/12122019131603_BALANCE%20AGRARIO%202019.pdf) (SEARCH-ONLY). The Argentine citrus association says world lemon area doubled in the last two decades, all producers grew area by at least 20%, volume rose in Spain, Turkey and above all South Africa, and the overproduction crisis "started in 2019"; Argentina lost ~10,000 of ~55,000 ha. — [Portal Fruticola, 18 Jan 2024 (VERIFIED)](https://www.portalfruticola.com/?p=549198). An earlier lemon glut (2002) was attributed to weather (no frost, rain), Turkish lira devaluation, Argentine arrivals and expiry of EU processing subsidies, explicitly not new plantations. — [Consumer.es, 7 Apr 2002 (VERIFIED)](https://www.consumer.es/?p=160252)
- Almond: sector bodies estimate ~645,964 ha in production in 2026/27, >36,000 ha of newly producing area, harvest forecast ~135,250 t kernel 2026/27 (another estimate up to 146,000 t), ASAJA reports price ~25% below last year; commentary says oversupply risk is structural if new plantings enter production together. — [Portal Fruticola](https://www.portalfruticola.com/?p=617192), [Commodity-board](https://commodity-board.com/la-cosecha-de-almendra-en-espana-superara-las-135-000-t-en-2026-27), [Agronews CyL](https://www.agronewscastillayleon.com/almendra-espana-2026-record-146000-toneladas-retos-mercado) (all SEARCH-ONLY, trade press, figures differ between sources)
- **(MY COMPUTATION, Eurostat Spain annual 2000-2024; method in Appendix).** Price = Eurostat first-marketing-stage price deflated by Spanish HICP; area/production = Eurostat apro_cpsh1.
  - Real-price dispersion (2015 EUR per 100 kg, 25 years): lettuce p10/p50/p90 = 24.3 / 38.9 / 51.9 (CV 0.30, sd of annual dlnP 0.26); cauliflower+broccoli 39.0 / 48.5 / 56.0 (CV 0.18, sd 0.22); melon 27.3 / 34.0 / 41.7 (CV 0.16, sd 0.22); almond 78.5 / 102.9 / 171.0 (CV 0.30, sd 0.22); lemon 22.3 / 32.3 / 50.7 (CV 0.33, sd 0.38).
  - Area vs price, first differences `dlnP ~ dlnArea`: lettuce +1.25 +/- 1.66, cauliflower+broccoli -0.16 +/- 0.80, melon -0.62 +/- 0.83, almond +0.33 +/- 0.86, lemon +1.72 +/- 1.49, orange +2.16 +/- 2.60, mandarin -1.89 +/- 2.18 (R2 <= 0.06 everywhere): **no detectable area effect, and wrong signs for several crops** (area is a poor proxy of supply; production includes yield shocks).
  - Production vs price, first differences `dlnP ~ dlnProduction`: melon -1.15 +/- 0.55 (R2 0.16), almond -0.29 +/- 0.12 (R2 0.23), lemon -0.28 +/- 0.22, lettuce -0.16 +/- 0.96, cauliflower+broccoli -0.19 +/- 0.82. Only melon and almond are individually significant; they bracket the plausible national flexibility (-0.3 to -1.2).
  - "Area anomaly" test (the proposed index): ln(area_t / mean area of previous 5 yrs) vs real price gap to its prior-5-yr mean: lettuce +0.05 +/- 0.98, cauliflower+broccoli -0.43 +/- 0.59, melon -0.02 +/- 0.58, almond +0.64 +/- 1.18, orange -0.89 +/- 1.17, mandarin -1.14 +/- 0.84, lemon +0.30 +/- 1.25. Inverse-variance pooled slope about **-0.28 +/- 0.31** (hand-computed from these slopes; naive SE, understated because 5-year windows overlap and crops are correlated).
  - Cobweb test `dlnArea_t ~ dlnP_(t-1)`: cauliflower+broccoli +0.14 +/- 0.05 (R2 0.23; a 10% price rise predicts +1.4% area next year), all other crops |coef| <= 0.04 and insignificant.
  - Perennial trends: almond area 527 kha (2014) to 663 kha (2024) (+26%), real price 150 to 84 EUR/100 kg 2015-base (-44%); lemon area 38.5 kha (2015) to 48.3 kha (2024) (+25%), real price 53.1 to 25.0 (-53%), the lowest of the series. Consistent with slow structural oversupply but confounded by world supply and consumption (cannot separate).
  - Calendar-year Eurostat data blur campaigns: lemon production peaked in 2018 (1,087 kt, price 50.5) and the price collapse appears in 2019 (34.9, production 885 kt) because the 2018/19 campaign straddles two calendar years. The ASAJA "+36.5% production in 2019" quote does not match Eurostat (2019 production fell vs 2018); treat as a different perimeter (campaign or Alicante) and do not use.

### Inferences
- Textbook cobweb stability (supply elasticity / demand elasticity > 1 gives divergent cycles) applies to SE Spain greenhouse crops in the 1975-99 data, but today's market has EU-wide supply, imports, programmed contracts and Murcian staggered planting windows, which should damp pure annual cycles. I found no study showing a clean hog-cycle in Murcian open-field lettuce/broccoli; the claim "if everyone plants lettuce the price falls" is plausible theory, weakly supported at national annual level by the data I could test.
- Because farm-gate demand is derived from retail demand, with a roughly constant absolute marketing margin the farm-level demand elasticity is *smaller* in absolute value than the retail one (Gardner-type derived demand), so farm-gate price flexibility is *larger* than the retail flexibility. This is textbook, not cited here; it is why the Galdeano demand elasticities at farm prices (-0.06 to -0.34, i.e. flexibility -3 to -17 if the market were closed) are far steeper than my empirical production flexibilities (-0.3 to -1.2). The gap itself is the evidence that the EU open market, substitutes and storage/exports dampen the effect: prior for the national flexibility phi in [-0.3, -3], central about -1 (my judgement).
- For a 20-30% area anomaly (large; Spanish broccoli+cauliflower area rose 32.3 to 40.4 kha in 2015-2018, +25%, while real price rose, which is not what an oversupply story predicts) the price effect is second-order versus weather/EU supply.
- For perennials the right oversupply signal is the **pipeline**: area planted but not yet bearing, plus world supply, not annual declared area.

### Gaps
- No Murcia/Campo-de-Cartagena open-field lettuce or broccoli supply-response estimate; no own-price elasticity at farm-gate for lettuce, broccoli, melon, watermelon, artichoke found (Okrent-Alston commodity table and the ERS elasticities database not opened; CTIFL value is an aggregate from a snippet).
- The Galdeano-Gomez demand elasticities (-0.06 to -0.34) are at regional farm-price level for 1975-1999 greenhouse crops and not transferable to today's EU open-field market; the 2003 flexibilities are normalised-price coefficients, not directly usable as an ordinary price flexibility.
- My regressions are national annual, n about 15-25, with overlapping windows, no control for demand/EU supply/weather, calendar-year aggregation, and broccoli+cauliflower combined (and Eurostat melon area falls from 41.6 kha in 2000 to 16.9 kha in 2024, partly maybe definitional). They show "no detectable effect", not "no effect".
- Artichoke and broccoli (separate) have no Eurostat price series; watermelon price exists (code 04199914) but I did not regress it; citrus other than orange/mandarin/lemon not checked.
- Weekly or monthly supply data (planting-date to harvest-week) for the comarca does not exist in the open sources found, so intra-season staggering (the Cajamar mechanism) cannot be modelled.

## Q2. Is the Campo de Cartagena market local or European, and at what scale should "oversupply" be measured?

### Takeaway
European. Spain supplies ~42% of EU lettuce and over one-third of EU cauliflower/broccoli, extra-EU imports are under 1% of EU output, Murcia is roughly half of Spanish lettuce area and ~65-68% of Spanish lettuce exports, and Germany/France/UK/Netherlands/Italy are the destinations. So price is set in EU-wide spot markets in the sales window; the relevant supply is "Spain + Murcia winter window" and the relevant oversupply scale is **national (MAPA) or Murcia-wide declared area for the crop, with the comarca weighted by its share**, not the comarca alone.

### Cited Findings
- Eurostat-based calculation (FreshPlaza, 26 Jun 2026): Spain = 42% of EU lettuce output (2025) and more than one-third of EU cauliflower/broccoli; EU lettuce output -5.2% and cauliflower/broccoli -9.6% since 2017 while Spain's lettuce is about -1% and cauliflower/broccoli +5.6%; imports from outside the EU equal only 0.7% (lettuce) and 0.5% (cauliflower/broccoli) of EU output. Spanish broccoli figures include romanesco (comparability caveat). — [FreshPlaza (VERIFIED)](https://www.freshplaza.com/europe/article/9851004/eu-harvest-of-open-field-vegetables-declines-but-self-sufficiency-remains-strong/). A different summary quotes 50.5% of EU lettuce in 2025 (HortiDaily-based); the two figures conflict — [Pulse summary](https://www.pulse.bot/agriculture/news/spanish-lettuce-dominates-eu-markets-60ba5572-aec7-4192-a770-54395eb46e9d/) (SEARCH-ONLY)
- Murcia lettuce: about 16,000 of >35,000 ha of Spanish lettuce are in Murcia; Murcia = 65% of Spanish lettuce exports (another report: 510,000 of 760,000 t, 68%); of Murcian lettuce exports Germany 30%, France 18%, UK 12%; 80% of exported lettuce is consumed in the EU. — [Ecomercio Agrario: Murcia lidera la exportacion de lechuga](https://ecomercioagrario.com/?p=141190), [Ecomercio Agrario (lettuce 2nd most exported)](https://ecomercioagrario.com/?p=103836) (SEARCH-ONLY, years unclear, mixed area/volume/value). MAPA 2011-era snippet (from the existing note): Campo de Cartagena 15,420 ha lettuce = 46% of Spain, broccoli 10,618 ha.
- Broccoli: Murcia exported 254,000 t of broccoli in 2023 and supplied seven of every ten Spanish broccoli exports; 2014 data: Murcia 52% of Spanish broccoli, 40% of Spanish lettuce. — [Tridge](https://www.tridge.com/ko/news/the-region-of-murcia-the-main-producer-and-e-zszdwm), [Fructidor](https://fructidor.com/en/news/news-detail/a468fa62-042f-489c-b1c4-864e0989e1ea) (SEARCH-ONLY)
- 2018 Murcian fruit and vegetable exports: Germany 25% (627 M EUR, 662,905 t), UK 23.2%, France 16.6%; brassicas (incl. broccoli) second category by value, 336.3 M EUR. — [Alimarket](https://www.alimarket.es/alimentacion/noticia_amp/294722/murcia-batio-su-record-de-exportacion-hortofruticola-en-2018) (SEARCH-ONLY)
- Cajamar: Spain's "leadership in supplying Europe with lettuce in the winter months" plus staggered and in places lower supply explains the +21.5% value of lettuce exports Sep 2021-Feb 2022 (515.9 M EUR; Germany +22.6%, UK +22.1%, France +18.2%, Italy +38.9%, Netherlands +19.2% in value). — [Cajamar C1 2022 (VERIFIED)](https://publicacionescajamar.es/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/horticolas-aire-libre-c1-2022.pdf)
- Headline "Murcia, the only open-field vegetable production area in winter" (EU context) — [Fruit Today](https://fruittoday.com/en/murcia-the-only-open-field-vegetable-production-area-in-winter/) (SEARCH-ONLY, title only; body not read)
- Spanish tomato chain: consumer prices respond faster and more strongly to producer-price shocks than the reverse (threshold VAR) — [CIHEAM/NEWMEDIT 2026](https://newmedit.ciheam.org/2026/03/10/is-there-a-variation-between-farmer-perceived-and-consumer-paid-tomato-prices-in-spain-a-tvar-model-approach/) (SEARCH-ONLY); relevant only as a note that farm-gate prices are the residual in the chain.

### Inferences
- Rough comarca share of EU supply (my estimate, unverified chain): Spain 42% x Murcia about 48% of Spanish lettuce area (16/33.2 kha, using Eurostat 2024 area 32.8 kha) = about 20% of EU lettuce on an annual-production basis; the Campo de Cartagena is the bulk of Murcian lettuce, and in Nov-Mar Murcia has few EU open-field competitors, so the effective share in the winter sales window is higher (maybe 30-50%, unverified). That makes the comarca a price-influencing block in winter, but only for that window.
- One farmer (a few ha of 16,000 ha) has no measurable price effect: the oversupply term is a collective-behaviour indicator, not a marginal-revenue correction for the user's own parcel. Present it as "crowded crop" risk, not as the farmer's own impact.
- Scale choice: use the **Murcia-region (or national) declared area for the crop** as the numerator of the anomaly index when a comarca baseline is unavailable, and weight by the comarca share s_c for the price effect. For perennials (lemon, almond, citrus) use national or world (Argentina, Turkey, South Africa, California) supply, comarca irrelevant.
- Design risk (my inference, uncited): if the tool is adopted by many farmers it reads the same area index and recommends the same crop to all, creating the herding it tries to prevent; mitigate by capping the recommendation, adding own-app recommendations to the area index, or randomising among near-tied options.

### Gaps
- No verified current share of Campo de Cartagena within Murcia or within EU for lettuce/broccoli/melon/artichoke; no EU weekly price formation study (Mercamurcia, German wholesale links). EU Fruit and Vegetable market observatory not searched. All Murcia export shares are from trade press with unstated years.
- Whether Campo de Cartagena prices differ from national/EU prices (basis) was not tested.

## Q4. How existing studies and tools model saturation

### Takeaway
The established method in agricultural economics is an area-response (supply) function with expected price plus a downward-sloping demand/inverse-demand curve (Nerlove partial adjustment, cobweb), and in farm planning, price variability enters through scenarios and CVaR rather than through an explicit area-dependent price. I did not find a published crop-recommendation tool that models market saturation with an explicit area term; the whiteboard idea is a reasonable simplification of the inverse-demand approach.

### Cited Findings
- Inverse demand: price (normalised) as a function of quantities supplied, with flexibilities as the key parameters, applied to Spanish fruit and vegetables — [Galdeano-Gomez & Jaen-Garcia 2003 (VERIFIED)](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/184184/files/pdf_reeap-r198_04.pdf).
- Area response: `log S_t = a + b log P_expected + c log S_(t-1)`, short-run b and long-run b/(1-c) — [Galdeano-Gomez 2001 (VERIFIED)](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/28742/files/01020021.pdf).
- Farm-level crop selection under price variability via MILP maximising expected profit or CVaR, using historical price scenarios, with no endogenous area-price link — [Filippi, Mansini & Stevanato 2017](https://iris.unibs.it/handle/11379/487654) (SEARCH-ONLY); CVaR as an LP over scenarios — [Rockafellar & Uryasev](https://sites.math.washington.edu/~rtr/papers/rtr179-CVaR1.pdf). Details in `prices-and-decision-layer.md` Q3.
- A multi-objective rotation MILP finds diversity can rise 60% for under 5% income loss (snippet) — see `prices-and-decision-layer.md` Q3; relevant to the diversification/portfolio argument — [multi-objective rotation record](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c333t-4559c202ac6cd1131b1ee7a6a991ccf97dfb23a09c14198b45881c70b49554443/Details) (SEARCH-ONLY).

### Inferences
- Three implementable saturation models, ranked by explainability: (a) anomaly index SI = area / 5-yr mean mapped to a price shift (proposed below); (b) inverse demand `p = p0 (Q/Q0)^phi` with phi the price flexibility (equivalent to (a) in logs: ln p = ln p0 + phi ln(Q/Q0)); (c) price percentile conditional on high-area years (non-parametric). Option (c) is not feasible with the data: about 20-25 national annual observations, and my own tests show no detectable relation, so conditioning would just be noise.
- A diminishing-returns term `1 - k (SI-1)^+` is the same idea with an arbitrary slope; replace k by an interpretable, sourced phi x s.
- Portfolio/diversification: the existing CVaR/MC layer already rewards crops whose price paths are not synchronous; add a concentration cap (e.g. recommendation list must not put all parcels in one crop) rather than relying only on the area term.

### Gaps
- No search covering crop-recommendation apps/papers (e.g., agri advisory tools, FAO tools) for saturation modelling; this statement is "not found", not "does not exist". `prior-art-and-stack.md` may cover tools.

## Q5. Recommended oversupply term, uncertainty display, and interaction with CVaR / Monte Carlo

### Takeaway
Replace the multiplier `(1 - oversupply)` by a **price-scenario shift**: `P_adj = P_draw x exp(beta x a)` where `a = ln(area_now / mean area of the previous 5 years)` (the "area anomaly") and `beta = phi x s` is an uncertain, sourced coefficient (central about -0.3, plausible range -0.9 to 0). Draw beta per Monte Carlo path, show the effect with and without it, and flag crops with a >1.15 anomaly as "crowded". It will move prices by single-digit percentages for typical anomalies, which is the honest size of the effect.

### Cited Findings
(This section is mostly design; the quantitative anchors are the cited/computed items in Q1-Q2.)
- Anchors: price flexibility to production at national level -0.29 +/- 0.12 (almond) and -1.15 +/- 0.55 (melon) (MY COMPUTATION, Q1); pooled 5-yr-anomaly slope -0.28 +/- 0.31 (MY COMPUTATION, Q1); Spanish short-run area elasticity to expected price 0.4 to 1.5 and farm-gate demand elasticity -0.06 to -0.34 (greenhouse, 1975-99) — [Galdeano-Gomez 2001](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/28742/files/01020021.pdf); EU-wide market with <1% extra-EU imports — [FreshPlaza/Eurostat](https://www.freshplaza.com/europe/article/9851004/eu-harvest-of-open-field-vegetables-declines-but-self-sufficiency-remains-strong/).
- Weekly CVaR/MC machinery is already specified (sample whole historical price years, alpha 0.8 for 10-15 scenarios, risk slider lambda) — [prices-and-decision-layer.md Q1, Q3](prices-and-decision-layer.md).

### Inferences (design proposal; every parameter below is a judgement flagged for the team to adjust)
**1. Index.** `SI_c = A_c,now / mean(A_c, t-5..t-1)`, `a_c = ln SI_c`, clipped to [-0.3, +0.5]. Use declared area (FEGA comarca now vs last year) for display and national MAPA/Eurostat area for the 5-year baseline. If only 2 FEGA years exist, compute a_c from MAPA/ESYRCE Murcia area history instead and show FEGA as a cross-check. Perennials: use `a_c = ln((bearing + non-bearing area) / bearing area)` (pipeline) when available, else national area anomaly.

**2. Price shift.** `P_adj,c = P_draw,c x exp(beta_c x a_c)`, `beta_c = phi_c x s_c`.
- phi_c (market price flexibility): prior Uniform(-1.5, -0.3) for vegetables from my national production regressions (almond -0.29, melon -1.15, midpoint about -0.7) and the plausible upper range from low farm-gate demand elasticity (see Q1 inference); central -0.7 to -1.
- s_c (share of the sales-window supply that the comarca controls, the "dilution"): 0.2 for lettuce/broccoli annual basis, up to 0.4 for the winter window; set to 1.0 only if the anomaly is computed from national area (then the market anomaly itself is the driver).
- Resulting central beta about -0.2 to -0.3 for comarca-based anomaly and about -0.7 to -1 for a national-area anomaly. Example: national lettuce area +10% above 5-yr mean gives about -7% price at the central value, range 0 to -15%; compared with annual price sd 0.26 that is a modest shift, and the uncertainty band is wider than the shift itself (be upfront).
- Sign and symmetry: apply symmetric (an unusually small area raises price) but cap the bonus at +10% so the score does not reward scarcity speculation.

**3. Interaction with the Monte Carlo / CVaR.**
- Each MC path: pick a historical price year (preserves cross-crop correlation and seasonal shape), draw `beta` once per path (common across crops through the shared `phi` draw, plus per-crop `s_c`), compute P_adj, then success %, revenue, margin. The reported "rotation B wins in X% of scenarios" then includes oversupply parameter uncertainty automatically.
- Because the shift is multiplicative on price and a_c is known at decision time, it moves the whole distribution (mean and tail) of the crowded crop down; with CVaR the effect on the tail is amplified in absolute EUR for high-price crops. Show both E and CVaR with the term on/off.
- Do NOT also multiply the final score by `(1 - oversupply)`: that double counts; the price already carries it. Keep "success %" independent (agronomic) as agreed in D-005.
- Lag: historical price years already embed some average oversupply (high-area years inside the sample). The shift is therefore a deviation from typical crowding, so use the anomaly (relative to 5-yr mean), not the absolute area; absolute-area penalties would double count against the same history.

**4. How to show the uncertainty to a non-technical farmer.** (a) A traffic light on "Crowded crop?" from SI: green < 1.05, amber 1.05-1.2, red > 1.2 (thresholds are my judgement, calibrate to the observed SI distribution, e.g. top quintile); (b) one sentence: "This year the comarca has X% more area of lettuce than the 5-year average. In past years that kind of surplus has meant lower prices, but the effect is hard to separate from weather, we estimate between 0% and -15%"; (c) a price band chart with two bars (with/without the oversupply shift), the gap being the oversupply effect; (d) a tooltip with the honest caveat "the market is European; what other farmers plant matters less than the weather and German demand" and "consult your technician".

**5. Validation you can do in 1-2 hours.** Re-run the Appendix regression on the MAPA/ESAM weekly series once transcribed (more observations than 25 annual points): regress the price gap at harvest week on SI; if the pooled slope remains insignificant, keep beta prior but state "no statistically detectable effect in our data" on the slide. That sentence is defensible and rare among competitors.

### Gaps
- phi and s_c are priors from mixed evidence, not estimates for Campo de Cartagena. No data on planting-date distribution, so intra-season crowding is not modelled. The herding risk of a widely used tool is unquantified.

## Appendix: reproducing my Eurostat analysis (about 40 lines of Python)
1. Download (no key): `apro_cpsh1?format=JSON&lang=en&geo=ES&sinceTimePeriod=1999&crops=V2300&crops=V1100&crops=V2800&crops=V3510&crops=V3520&crops=V3600&crops=T3000&crops=F4300&crops=T1000&crops=T2000&crops=V3100`; `apri_ap_crpouta?format=JSON&lang=en&geo=ES&sinceTimePeriod=2000`; `prc_hicp_aind?format=JSON&lang=en&geo=ES&coicop=CP00&unit=INX_A_AVG&sinceTimePeriod=1999` (base `https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/api/dissemination/statistics/1.0/data/`).
2. Decode the JSON-stat cube (dimensions `freq, crops, strucpro, geo, time`; `value` is a flat dict keyed by row-major position). Use `strucpro=AR_THS_HA` (area) and `HPRD_HUMD_EU_THS_T` (production).
3. Real price = price / HICP x 100. Variables: `p=ln(real price)`, `a=ln(area)`, `q=ln(production)`.
4. Regressions (OLS, numpy `lstsq`): (i) `dp ~ dq`; (ii) `dp ~ da`; (iii) anomaly test: `ln(area_t / mean(area_{t-5..t-1}))` against `p_t - mean(p_{t-5..t-1})`; (iv) cobweb `da_t ~ dp_{t-1}`; also levels with a linear trend.
5. Pool slopes with inverse-variance weights for the single beta prior. Limitations as listed under Q1 Gaps (n small, overlapping windows, no controls, calendar vs campaign year).
